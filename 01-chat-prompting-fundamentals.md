## 🧠 THE WHAT

# WHO. WHAT. FORMAT. REVISE.

Four words. That's the whole skill this module teaches. Every strong prompt you'll ever write has these four things in it, and almost every weak prompt is missing at least one. Keep these four words in your head, you'll use them for the rest of this course.

### The One-Sentence Version

> Claude answers the question you actually asked, not the question you meant to ask. So the fix for a bad answer is almost never "try a different tool," it's "add the missing context," and WHO, WHAT, FORMAT, REVISE is how you find what's missing.

### Why This Module Comes First

Every other Chat skill in this course, refining a draft, using Projects, chaining prompts together, assumes you can already do this one. This is the foundation everything else stands on.

### 💡 The Core Idea

Claude only knows what's in your message, nothing more. Anything you leave unsaid, it has to guess at, and it guesses toward "generic" every time.

**Analogy:** Think of Claude as a brilliant substitute teacher walking into your classroom for the first time. They're capable, but they don't know your students, your routines, or what "good" looks like in your room. If you just say "teach a lesson," you'll get something generic, and reasonably so. If you brief them the way you'd brief a real substitute, here's the group, here's where we are, here's what I need, they can do the job well.

Claude works the same way. It isn't reading your mind, it's reading your message. A short, vague message gets a short, vague answer, not because the tool is weak, but because that's the only reasonable response to an underspecified request.

### WHO. WHAT. FORMAT. REVISE. — In Practice

| The word | What it means | Weak prompt | Strong prompt |
|---|---|---|---|
| **WHO** | Who it's for | "students" | "7th graders still shaky on fractions with different denominators" |
| **WHAT** | What you actually want | "help with a lesson plan" | "30-minute lesson: 5-min warm-up, one guided practice, 3-question exit ticket" |
| **FORMAT** | The shape of the answer | (unstated) | "as a table" / "as a bulleted list" / "as a short paragraph" |
| **REVISE** | Room to go back and adjust | treating the first answer as final | "make this shorter" / "too advanced, simplify" / "add two examples" |

### The One Habit That Fixes Most Bad Results

**Try this:** When a response feels generic, stop and ask yourself: *what do I know right now that Claude doesn't?* Say that thing out loud, or type it into your next message. This single habit resolves the majority of frustrating, forgettable Claude answers.

### ✅ Quick Check

Before moving to the transfer stories, try rewriting this real prompt to include the four elements above: *"Write a rubric for my students' presentations."* If you can name who the students are, what the presentation is about, what format you want the rubric in, and what you'd ask for in a second round of revision, you've got the skill.

<details>
<summary><strong>See a strong answer</strong></summary>

> "Write a rubric for 9th grade students presenting a 5-minute persuasive speech. Score on four categories: content, organization, delivery, and use of evidence, each out of 5 points. Format it as a table I can print. Once you've made it, I'll probably ask you to simplify the language in one or two categories."

Notice what's doing the work here: **WHO** (9th graders, persuasive speech), **WHAT** (specific scoring categories and point values, not just "a rubric"), **FORMAT** (a printable table), and **REVISE** (naming in advance that a second pass is expected). If your rewrite hit even three of these four, you've got the habit. The fourth will come naturally the more you use it.

## 🔄 THE HOW

---

### 🧭 "The Quiz Nobody Could Use"
**For classroom teachers, any grade level**

A middle school science teacher, three preps deep into a Tuesday, opens Claude fifteen minutes before class and types: "make a quiz on the water cycle." What comes back is technically correct and completely wrong for her room: too many questions, vocabulary two grade levels too high for her inclusion section, no answer key.

She rewrites it: "Make a 5-question multiple-choice quiz on the water cycle for 6th graders, including students with reading accommodations. Include an answer key, 5th grade reading level." The second version is usable in under a minute.

**Nothing about Claude changed between attempts. What changed was that she stopped assuming Claude could see her classroom, and started describing it.**

*Research note: this is one of the most commonly documented AI frustrations among teachers, generic or unusable output traced directly back to missing grade-level and context detail in the original request, not a limitation of the tool itself.*

---

### 🧭 "The Copy-Paste Trap"
**For anyone who's never quite trusted their own prompts**

A first-year teacher spends a Sunday night googling "best AI prompts for teachers" and copies a template word for word, swapping in his topic. It works okay. He repeats this every week, always searching for someone else's prompt first.

One week he can't find a template for what he actually needs, an oddly specific parent email about a group project dispute, so he just describes the situation in his own words. It works better than any template he'd copied all month.

**He'd been treating prompting like a password he had to look up, when it was really just clear communication, the same skill he already uses explaining something to a colleague.**

---

### 🧭 "Briefing, Not Ordering"
**For team leads, small business owners, anyone managing people**

A small business owner asks Claude to "write our new-hire onboarding checklist," gets something generic back, and tells her business partner AI "isn't that useful." Weeks later, she watches a newer employee use Claude for a client proposal. He never expects the first draft to be final, he goes back and forth three or four times, tightening it each round, the way he'd guide a junior colleague's first attempt at something.

**She'd been treating Claude like a vending machine: put in a request, expect a finished product. It works more like briefing a new hire: explain, review, give specific feedback, revise.**

*Research note: "confusing a prompt with a process," expecting a single instruction to fully replace ongoing direction, is a documented, named mistake among business leaders new to AI tools.*

---

### 🧭 "The Skill You Already Have"
**For teachers thinking about management, or managers thinking about teaching**

A veteran teacher, years into directing a room of twenty-five teenagers, differentiating instruction, redirecting without shutting a kid down, explaining the same concept three different ways until it lands, starts using Claude for lesson planning. She notices the skill that gets her good results from Claude is nearly identical to her best classroom-management instincts: be specific about who you're talking to, adjust for what they already know, give precise feedback instead of vague feedback, and don't expect perfect understanding on the first pass.

**If she ever moves from the classroom into corporate training or team leadership, this isn't a new skill to learn. She's already been running it on twenty-five teenagers at a time, for years.**
