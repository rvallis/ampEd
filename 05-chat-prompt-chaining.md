## 🧠 THE WHAT

# ONE BIG ASK BECOMES A FEW SMALL ONES.

Seven words. When a task feels too big for a single prompt, it usually is, and the fix is to split it into a short sequence instead.

### The One-Sentence Version

> Asking for an entire finished project in one message often produces something overwhelming, inconsistent, or shallow, asking for it in stages produces something you'd actually use.

### The Core Idea

There's a real, common mistake: asking for something like "an entire unit plan with lessons, activities, rubrics, and assessments" all in one message. Trying to do too much at once tends to make the response overwhelming, inconsistent, or repetitive, since Claude is trying to hold five different jobs in its head at the same time instead of doing one well.

**Analogy:** think about the difference between asking a new employee to "build the entire onboarding program" versus asking them to first draft an outline, then, once you've approved it, build out week one, then week two. The second approach isn't slower, it's actually faster overall, because each piece gets built on a foundation you've already checked and approved, instead of everything getting built at once on an assumption that might be wrong.

### What Chaining Actually Looks Like

| Step | Example |
|---|---|
| **1. Start small** | "Create an outline for a 2-week unit on fractions for 4th grade." |
| **2. Approve or adjust** | "This looks right, but move the review day earlier." |
| **3. Expand one piece at a time** | "Now expand lesson 1 into a full detailed plan with activities." |
| **4. Repeat for the next piece** | "Now do the same for lesson 2." |

Each step builds on something you already checked, instead of hoping a single giant prompt got everything right at once.

### Quick Check

You want a full week of differentiated reading activities across three ability levels. Should that be one prompt or a chain?

<details>
<summary><strong>See a strong answer</strong></summary>

> A chain. Start with an outline of the week's structure and approve it, then ask for each ability level's activities one at a time, or each day one at a time. A single all-in-one prompt asking for a full week across three levels is exactly the kind of request that tends to come back overwhelming, inconsistent, or shallow in one or more of the levels.

The tell is complexity stacking on complexity: multiple days, multiple levels, multiple activity types, all at once. Any time you're listing several distinct components in a single request, that's the signal to split it.

</details>

## 🔄 THE HOW

---

### "The Unit Plan That Actually Worked, Once She Stopped Asking for All of It at Once"
**For classroom teachers, any grade level**

A teacher's first attempt at AI-assisted unit planning was one giant prompt: an entire two-week fractions unit, with lessons, activities, rubrics, and assessments, all at once. What came back was messy, some days barely connected to others, activities that didn't build on each other. Her second attempt started with just an outline, which she reviewed and adjusted, then she asked for lesson 1 in full detail, checked it, then lesson 2. The final unit was coherent in a way the first attempt never was.

**Nothing about her prompting skill from earlier modules had gotten worse. The task itself was just too big for one message to carry well.**

*Research note: this exact mistake, trying to generate an entire complex deliverable in a single prompt, is a specifically documented and named error, and the fix, breaking big tasks into smaller steps, is the standard, research-backed correction.*

---

### "Building the Proposal in Pieces, Not All at Once"
**For freelancers, consultants, and small business owners**

A consultant used to ask Claude for a "complete client proposal" in one shot, executive summary, scope, timeline, and pricing all at once, and got back something generic in every section, since none of it got real attention. Splitting it, "just the executive summary first," then approving it before moving to scope, then timeline, then pricing, took a little longer step by step but produced something sharper in every section, because each piece got Claude's full attention instead of a fifth of it.

**She wasn't asking for less. She was asking for the same amount, in an order that actually worked.**

---

### "Directing a Big Project the Same Way You'd Direct a New Hire"
**For teachers thinking about management, or managers thinking about teaching**

A department head realized that the way she chains prompts together for a big Claude task is identical to how she'd delegate a big project to a new team member: never "go build the whole thing," always "start with an outline, show me, then we'll go section by section." Neither a new employee nor Claude does their best work when handed an enormous, multi-part task with no checkpoints. Both do better broken into stages with a quick check-in between each one.

**The chaining skill isn't really about Claude. It's project management, applied to a conversation instead of a team.**
