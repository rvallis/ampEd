## 🧠 THE WHAT

# UPLOAD IT, DON'T RETYPE IT.

Five words. If you're copying information out of a document by hand so you can describe it to Claude in a message, stop, upload the document instead.

### The One-Sentence Version

> Claude can read what you give it, so the fastest path to a useful answer is usually handing over the real document, not a summary of it you typed from memory.

### The Core Idea

A huge amount of wasted time in any job that involves paperwork, IEPs, spreadsheets, reports, contracts, comes from re-typing or re-describing information that already exists in a file. Claude can read PDFs, spreadsheets, and other documents directly. That means the fastest, most accurate way to get help with something is usually to hand over the real file, not a secondhand description of it.

**Analogy:** think about the difference between telling a colleague "the spreadsheet has some numbers that look off, columns C and D I think" versus just handing them the spreadsheet. One is a guessing game. The other lets them actually see what's there. Claude works the same way, a real file gives it something concrete to work from instead of your best paraphrase of it.

### ⚠️ One Real Caution Before You Upload

If a document contains sensitive personal information, student names tied to disability status, medical details, financial account numbers, stop and think before uploading it as-is. This isn't a hypothetical concern, it's an active caution researchers and practitioners raise specifically around uploading documents like IEPs into AI tools: strip or replace identifying details where you can, and check what your school or organization's policy actually allows before uploading anything with a real student's name attached to sensitive information.

### Quick Check

You have a messy spreadsheet of quiz scores and want Claude's help spotting which students are falling behind. What's the smart first move, before you even open Claude?

<details>
<summary><strong>See a strong answer</strong></summary>

> Check whether the spreadsheet needs to be de-identified first, replacing names with student ID numbers or initials, depending on what your school's data policy actually allows, before uploading it anywhere. Only after that's settled does the actual Claude question matter: upload the file and ask something specific, like "which students have dropped more than 10 points over the last three quizzes."

The instinct to upload the real file instead of retyping numbers by hand is correct. The instinct to check what's actually appropriate to share first is just as important, and easy to skip when you're in a hurry.

</details>

## 🔄 THE HOW

---

### "The Spreadsheet That Used to Take an Hour to Read"
**For classroom teachers, any grade level**

A teacher used to sit down with a quiz-score spreadsheet every Friday and manually scan for students trending downward, a slow, error-prone process at the end of a long week. Uploading the actual spreadsheet and asking Claude to flag any student whose average dropped over the last three assessments turned an hour of squinting at rows into a two-minute check, and one she trusted more, since Claude wasn't getting tired by row 40 the way she was.

**The insight wasn't "AI can read spreadsheets." It was that she'd been doing manually what the file itself already contained the answer to.**

*Research note: special education teachers in particular report being overwhelmed by paperwork and data review, to the point that it crowds out actual instruction time, a documented, widely reported strain, not an exaggeration.*

---

### "Reading the Contract Instead of Skimming It"
**For freelancers, consultants, and small business owners**

A freelance consultant used to skim client contracts quickly, worried about missing a clause buried on page four but not having time to read every one carefully. Uploading the actual PDF and asking Claude to summarize the payment terms and flag anything unusual compared to standard freelance contracts gave her a second set of eyes on something she'd been rushing through alone.

**She wasn't replacing her own judgment. She was making sure nothing slipped past a tired read-through at 9pm.**

---

### "The IEP Nobody Had Time to Fully Read"
**For teachers thinking about management, or managers thinking about teaching**

A general education teacher, juggling five sections and a stack of IEPs for students she'd inherited mid-year, admits she used to skim straight to the accommodations page and hope she wasn't missing anything important elsewhere in the document. With appropriate de-identification and attention to her school's actual policy, uploading a document and asking Claude to summarize the key accommodations and any goals tied to her specific class gave her a faster, fuller read than skimming alone ever did.

**The problem was never that she didn't care. It was that a fifteen-page document and a five-minute planning period don't fit together, and a tool that can actually read the whole thing changes that math.**

*Research note: research on IEP implementation specifically identifies that teachers sometimes only read the accommodations section of a document, not because they don't care, but because time doesn't allow reading the entire file carefully, a documented gap between what's written and what's actually absorbed.*
