## 🧠 THE WHAT

# KNOW THE HANDOFF BEFORE YOU START.

Six words. Before you start a task that spans Claude and another tool, LMS, spreadsheet, email, decide where the handoff happens, so you're not improvising a copy-paste chain halfway through.

### The One-Sentence Version

> Claude rarely does the entire job by itself, so the real skill is knowing exactly what Claude should produce and exactly where that output needs to land next.

### The Core Idea

Context-switching between apps is one of the most consistently reported frustrations with AI tools, some studies put the average knowledge worker toggling between applications over a thousand times a day. Every time you copy something out of Claude and into another tool, there's a real cost: formatting breaks, you lose track of what's finished versus still in progress, and it's easy to paste the wrong version of something.

The fix isn't avoiding other tools, that's not realistic, you'll always need to get things into an LMS, a spreadsheet, an email. The fix is deciding, before you start, exactly what Claude's job is and exactly what the other tool's job is, so the handoff is a single clean step instead of a messy back-and-forth.

**Analogy:** think of a relay race. The runners don't just wander toward each other hoping to find the baton mid-stride, there's a marked handoff zone, both runners know exactly where it is, and the exchange happens cleanly because it was planned, not improvised.

### Planning the Handoff

| Question to ask first | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| What exactly should Claude produce? | A rubric? A table? Plain text? Knowing the end format up front avoids reformatting later |
| Where does it go next? | An LMS gradebook field? A spreadsheet column? An email body? |
| Does the format need to change to make that move? | A table in Claude might need to become a CSV, or plain text might need HTML formatting |

### Quick Check

You want Claude to draft five parent emails, then send them from your actual email account. What's the clean handoff plan?

<details>
<summary><strong>See a strong answer</strong></summary>

> Ask Claude to draft all five emails in plain text, clearly separated and labeled by student name, in one message. Then copy each one individually into your email client and send. The clean handoff here is: Claude produces finished, ready-to-send text, and your email tool's only job is sending, not editing or reformatting Claude's output.

The failure mode to avoid is going back and forth, asking for one email, copying it, coming back for edits, copying again, five separate times. Planning the full batch and the exact handoff format up front avoids that entirely.

</details>

### "The Grading Comments That Finally Stopped Getting Lost in Translation"
**For classroom teachers, any grade level**

A teacher used to ask Claude for grading comments one student at a time, copy each into her gradebook, then come back for the next, a slow, error-prone loop where she sometimes lost track of which comment belonged to which student. Once she planned the handoff properly, asking Claude for all comments in one batch, clearly labeled by student name, in a format she could paste directly into her gradebook, column matches, the whole process became a single clean copy instead of a dozen small ones.

**The problem was never that Claude's comments were bad. It was that she hadn't decided, up front, exactly what shape they needed to arrive in.**

*Research note: context-switching between AI tools and the other software people actually use to finish a task is one of the most consistently reported frustrations with AI adoption, described in workflow research as a genuine productivity drain, not a minor annoyance.*

---

### "Planning the Handoff Before Starting the Work, Not After"
**For freelancers, consultants, and small business owners**

A freelance consultant used to draft a proposal in Claude, then discover halfway through formatting it into her client-facing document tool that the structure didn't translate cleanly, headers in the wrong place, tables that needed to be rebuilt from scratch. Now she decides upfront: "I need this as a numbered list with these exact section headers, since that's what pastes cleanly into my template." The finished draft moves over in one clean step instead of a frustrating rebuild.

**She wasn't slowing down by planning the handoff first. She was avoiding the rebuild that used to happen every single time.**

---

### "Treating the Tool Switch Like a Shift Change"
**For teachers thinking about management, or managers thinking about teaching**

A manager compares a clean handoff between Claude and another tool to a well-run shift change at a job with rotating staff: the outgoing person doesn't just wander off, they hand over a clear, specific summary of exactly where things stand, so the next person can pick up cleanly. A sloppy handoff, vague, unclear what's finished, creates confusion and rework. A good one is quick precisely because it was planned.

**The tool switch isn't the hard part. Not planning for it is.**
