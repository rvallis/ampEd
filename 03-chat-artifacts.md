## 🧠 THE WHAT

# NOT A MESSAGE. A THING.

Four words. Most of what Claude gives you lives and dies in the conversation. An artifact doesn't, it becomes its own standalone piece you can view, edit, save, and use again, separate from the chat that created it.

### The One-Sentence Version

> If what you're asking for is really a document, a tool, or something you'll want to open again later, ask for it as an artifact, not as a paragraph buried in a conversation.

### The Core Idea

A regular Claude response lives in the chat. It's great for a quick answer, but if it's actually a worksheet, a rubric, a tracking spreadsheet, or a small interactive tool, leaving it as chat text means you'll be scrolling to find it again next week, or worse, retyping it from scratch.

**Analogy:** think of the difference between a sticky note and a filed document. A sticky note is fine for "call the front office at 2." It's a bad place to keep a rubric you'll use all semester. Claude can hand you either. An artifact is the filed document, it exists on its own, separate from the conversation, ready to be reopened, edited, and reused without you having to scroll back through old messages to find it.

### When to Ask for an Artifact

| Signal | Example |
|---|---|
| **You'll reuse it** | A rubric, a template, a tracking sheet you'll open again next week |
| **It's really a document, not an answer** | A parent newsletter, a syllabus, a printable handout |
| **It needs to stand alone** | A small interactive tool, a calculator, a formatted table too big for a chat bubble |
| **You want to keep editing it directly** | Going back and forth on a document without losing earlier versions in scroll-back |

### Quick Check

You ask Claude for five discussion questions about a short story. Should that be an artifact, or a regular chat answer?

<details>
<summary><strong>See a strong answer</strong></summary>

> Probably a regular chat answer, five discussion questions is quick to read right in the conversation and you likely won't need to reopen it as its own document. If it were a full discussion guide with questions, timing, and grouping instructions you'd print and reuse every year, that would tip toward an artifact.

The test isn't "is this good content," it's "will I want this as its own reusable thing, or is reading it once in the chat enough?"

</details>

## 🔄 THE HOW

---

### "The Rubric She Stopped Losing"
**For classroom teachers, any grade level**

A teacher used to ask Claude for a grading rubric fresh every time she needed one, scrolling back through old chats trying to find "the good one from last month," never quite finding it, usually just asking again from scratch. Once she started asking Claude to build it as an artifact instead, it became something she could actually reopen, tweak for the current unit, and keep using semester after semester, instead of a message buried somewhere in chat history she could never relocate.

**The content wasn't the problem. Where it lived was.**

---

### "The Tracker That Didn't Live in a Text Message"
**For freelancers, consultants, and small business owners**

A freelance consultant asked Claude to help track deliverables across three different clients, and got back a nicely formatted answer, in the chat, that she then had to manually copy into a spreadsheet anyway to actually use it day to day. The next time, she asked for the same tracker as an artifact instead, something she could open on its own, update directly, and refer back to without digging through a chat thread.

**She wasn't asking for different information. She was asking for it to exist somewhere she could actually use it.**

---

### "The Handout the Whole Department Started Using"
**For teachers thinking about management, or managers thinking about teaching**

A department head built a one-page onboarding guide for substitute teachers as an artifact, refined it over a few go-arounds, then shared the finished version with three other teachers in her department. None of them needed to see the conversation that created it, they just needed the finished thing. That's the real test of whether something should have been an artifact in the first place: could you hand it to someone else without also handing them the whole conversation around it?

**If the answer is yes, it probably should have been an artifact from the start.**

---

### One Last Thing

The skill from the last module, refining a first draft, applies just as well inside an artifact as inside a regular chat message. The difference here isn't how you improve it, it's where it lives once you're done.
