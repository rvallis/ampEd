## 🧠 THE WHAT

# LET IT ASK FIRST.

Four words. Instead of you doing all the explaining up front, ask Claude to interview you first, then act on what it learns.

### The One-Sentence Version

> When you don't know exactly what you need yet, or you're staring at a blank page, ask Claude to ask you questions first, one or two at a time, before it tries to produce anything.

### The Core Idea

Every other module in this branch has been about you giving Claude better instructions. This one flips it: sometimes the fastest way to a great result is to let Claude pull the details out of you, instead of you trying to guess what details matter before you've even started thinking clearly.

This isn't a trick or a workaround, it's a real, documented technique people describe as "reverse prompting" or "flipping the script": instead of prompting the AI, you get the AI to prompt you. The value is genuine and twofold. First, being asked good questions forces you to clarify your own thinking, so you're building from a real plan instead of typing whatever's in your head. Second, once Claude knows the specifics, its later work gets evaluated against your actual answers instead of a generic guess at what "good" looks like.

**Analogy:** think of a good doctor's intake appointment. They don't hand you a prescription the moment you sit down, they ask questions first, what hurts, since when, does anything make it better or worse, and only then do they act. The questions aren't a delay, they're what makes the eventual answer actually fit your situation instead of being a generic guess.

### How to Actually Use It

| Say this | What it does |
|---|---|
| "Ask me questions before you start, one or two at a time." | Slows Claude down to gather real information first |
| "Keep asking until you have enough to work with, then stop and tell me." | Prevents an endless interview with no end point |
| "Summarize what you've learned every few questions." | Lets you course-correct partway through, not just at the end |

### Quick Check

You have a vague sense you want to redesign your classroom's behavior management system, but no clear plan yet. Is this a good candidate for interview-me prompting, or should you write a detailed prompt yourself first?

<details>
<summary><strong>See a strong answer</strong></summary>

> A strong candidate for interview-me prompting. You don't have the plan yet, that's exactly the situation this technique is built for, letting good questions surface what you actually think before you've had to organize it yourself. If you already had a clear, detailed plan in your head, writing a strong direct prompt (using WHO, WHAT, FORMAT, REVISE) would likely be faster.

The signal to use this technique isn't "I have a hard task." It's "I don't yet know exactly what I want," which is a different problem entirely.

## 🔄 THE HOW

---

### "The Behavior Plan She Didn't Know She Had Opinions About"
**For classroom teachers, any grade level**

A teacher wanted to redesign her classroom behavior system but felt stuck every time she tried to just start writing a prompt about it, she wasn't even sure what she wanted yet. Asking Claude to interview her instead, one question at a time, "what's not working right now specifically," "what have you already tried," "what would success actually look like in three weeks," pulled a much clearer plan out of her than she could have written cold.

**She wasn't missing information. She was missing a structure for finding out what she already knew.**

*Research note: this exact pattern, being interviewed by the AI clarifies your own thinking before you've had to organize it yourself, is documented specifically as one of the real benefits of the technique, not just a nice side effect.*

---

### "Getting Better Feedback by Answering Questions First"
**For freelancers, consultants, and small business owners**

A consultant asked Claude to help refine her business's positioning statement, and instead of writing a long prompt describing her business herself, she asked Claude to interview her about it first. The questions it asked, "who specifically decides not to hire you, and why," surfaced gaps in her own thinking she hadn't noticed. When Claude finally drafted the positioning statement, it was built from her actual, specific answers, not a generic guess at what a consulting positioning statement usually sounds like.

**The interview wasn't a delay before the real work. It was the real work.**

---

### "Interviewing a New Hire Instead of Just Handing Them a Manual"
**For teachers thinking about management, or managers thinking about teaching**

A manager compares it to onboarding a promising but inexperienced new hire: you don't just hand them a manual and expect them to know exactly what matters to your team, you sit down and ask them questions, what's their background, where do they feel unsure, what do they already know. That short interview shapes everything that follows. Interview-me prompting does the same thing with Claude, letting the questions surface exactly the context that matters before any actual work begins.

**Good direction rarely starts with a monologue. It starts with a few good questions.**
