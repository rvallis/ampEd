## 🧠 THE WHAT

# FIRST DRAFT, NOT FINAL DRAFT.

Six words. If you remember nothing else from this module, remember that Claude's first response to anything is a rough draft, not a verdict, and treating it that way is the whole skill.

### The One-Sentence Version

> The gap between a mediocre Claude session and a great one usually isn't the first prompt, it's whether you sent a second one.

### The Core Idea

Claude's first response to any real request is built from your first message alone, and your first message almost never contains everything that matters. That's not a failure of the tool, it's just how a first draft works, human or AI. A first draft from a new colleague isn't something you sign off on, it's something you read and respond to: cut this, add that, make it sound less formal.

**Analogy:** Think about handing a stack of grading criteria to a substitute and asking them to write feedback for a class set of essays. Their first pass will probably be technically fine and also not quite you, too generic in the praise, too soft on a real problem, wrong tone for this particular group of kids. You wouldn't throw out the substitute or the whole idea, you'd read it and say "tighten this part, be more specific here, cut this line entirely." That's refinement, and it's exactly what a second message to Claude is doing.

### The Three Moves That Do Most of the Work

| Move | What it sounds like | When to use it |
|---|---|---|
| **Cut** | "Make this half as long." "Cut the intro." | Response is bloated, buried lede, too much throat-clearing |
| **Sharpen** | "Be more specific about X." "Give a real example, not a general one." | Response is technically correct but vague or generic |
| **Redirect** | "Actually, try this from a different angle." "That's not quite what I meant, here's what I actually need." | The whole approach missed the mark, not just the wording |

### Quick Check

Imagine Claude gives you a parent email that's accurate but sounds cold and overly formal. Write the single follow-up message you'd send to fix it, without starting over from scratch.

<details>
<summary><strong>See a strong answer</strong></summary>

> "This is accurate, but it reads too formal for this parent, we've talked casually before. Keep the same content but make the tone warmer, like you're talking to someone you know."

Notice what this does: it names what's wrong (tone, not content), it gives a clear direction (warmer, specific comparison), and it doesn't throw away the good parts by starting over. That's the whole move.

</details>

### "The Teachers Who Got Better Results Weren't Using a Different Tool"
**For classroom teachers, any grade level**

A district curriculum supervisor started reviewing lesson plans from teachers using AI tools weekly, and noticed a pattern: the strongest teachers never used what Claude gave them on the first try. They treated the first response as a starting point, revising learning goals for rigor, checking that examples fit their specific students, tightening anything vague. The teachers getting weaker results tended to take the first output essentially as-is.

**Same tool, same first prompt quality even, wildly different results, because one group stopped at draft one and the other group didn't.**

*Research note: this pattern, teachers who treat AI output as a first draft and revise it seeing meaningfully better outcomes than teachers who use it as-is, is a documented finding, not an assumption. It's one of the clearest, most consistent differences researchers have found between effective and ineffective classroom AI use.*

### "The Rubric That Took Four Tries, and Was Better For It"
**For anyone who gives up after one mediocre response**

A teacher asks Claude for a rubric, gets something usable but generic, and almost closes the tab thinking "that's fine, good enough." Instead, on a whim, she tries one more message: "This is solid, but score 'evidence' separately from 'organization,' they're getting blended right now." The next version is sharper. She pushes once more: "Now make the top-tier description more specific, right now it's just repeating the second tier with the word 'excellent' added."

Three short messages, maybe ninety seconds of typing, and the rubric goes from "fine" to "actually good."

**None of those three follow-ups required starting over. Each one kept what worked and fixed one specific thing.**

---

### "Why the Best Consultants Never Accept the First Draft, From Anyone"
**For freelancers, consultants, and small business owners**

A freelance consultant reviewing a junior colleague's first pass at a client report doesn't reject it and doesn't accept it blindly, she marks it up: this section's strong, this one needs a real number instead of "significant improvement," this paragraph can go entirely. She realizes, working with Claude on the same report later that day, she's doing the exact same thing, marking up a draft rather than either scrapping it or shipping it untouched.

**Whether the first draft came from a junior colleague or from Claude, the skill is identical: read it like an editor, not a customer.**
